# Coverage Patterns Reference

Use this reference when a task needs a complete storyboard coverage check: what camera roles are required, when to use one-take, when to cut, and how to combine macro narrative shots with close detail without changing the user's action, plot, lighting, environment, or style.

## Coverage Roles

Do not use every role by default. Select only the roles required by the user's scene.

Shot economy is part of directing. A complete plan is not the plan with the most shots; it is the plan where every necessary viewer question is answered with the fewest motivated camera changes.

| Role | Purpose | Typical camera choice |
|---|---|---|
| Establishing geography | Show where characters are, where danger/goal is, and what direction matters | extreme wide/wide, 18-28mm, fixed/slow move |
| Master action shot | Preserve full action continuity and body relation | wide or full-body medium wide, 24-35mm, stable screen direction |
| Moving follow | Keep a character's path readable through space | 24-35mm, tracking/gimbal/dolly, chest or waist height |
| Orbit-follow | Show a turn, circular movement, power shift, reveal, or surrounded relation | 28-40mm, controlled arc, maintain screen geography |
| Over-shoulder / relation shot | Show who faces whom and where the pressure comes from | 35-70mm, relation-aware |
| Reaction shot | Preserve emotional or tactical response without changing action | 50-85mm, close/medium close |
| Insert / detail proof | Show hand, foot, tool, prop, object, product, document mark, or contact point | 70-100mm, fixed/micro move |
| POV / subjective shot | Show what a character sees or targets | lens depends on subject, use only when viewpoint matters |
| Transition shot | Carry movement from one beat/location to the next | tracking, pan, foreground wipe, cut-on-motion |
| Ending hold | Let consequence, posture, or spatial result land | medium/wide, locked or settling camera |

## Director Viewpoint Progression

A storyboard is not complete just because it has wide, medium, and close shots. It needs a viewing strategy.

Choose a progression such as:

- **Pressure-first**: begin from the side that applies pressure, blocks space, or withholds information; then reveal the response; then return to a readable spatial shot.
- **Protagonist-first**: begin on the protagonist's listening, hand, foot, shoulder, tool, or hesitation; then let the outside force enter frame; then open the relation.
- **Spatial-first**: begin with macro geography; move into the path or relation; insert the decisive detail; return to spatial consequence.
- **Subjective-first**: begin from what a character sees, avoids, remembers, anticipates, or misunderstands; then cut to the objective result.
- **Process-first**: begin on a hand/tool/document/product operation; open to the person or system affected; return to proof or consequence.
- **Reveal-first**: begin with partial information, foreground occlusion, reflection, edge of frame, or back view; then reveal the actual relation or action.
- **One-take immersion**: begin in a motivated viewpoint, then shift distance/angle/focal feel inside the same shot as the scene changes.

Do not default to a neutral wide shot unless the viewer must first understand geography. Many scenes gain force from over-shoulder, low detail, foreground body/object, compressed distance, POV, reflection, or delayed reveal before the full space opens.

Avoid monotonous coverage ladders:

- Weak: neutral wide -> neutral medium wide -> neutral medium -> neutral medium-wide hold.
- Better: pressured viewpoint -> readable relation/action reveal -> insert/detail proof -> consequence shot.
- Better one-take: motivated partial viewpoint -> lateral or depth reveal -> tighter emphasis at the decisive beat -> controlled angle shift -> settle into consequence.

## One-Take vs Cutting

Before choosing shots, ask whether a cut improves understanding. If a cut only adds variety, do not cut. Convert the idea into a distance change, focus shift, small camera arc, lateral move, or held frame inside the same shot.

Prefer one-take when:

- full-body movement, footwork, dance, chase, walkthrough, process, group staging, or physical continuity is the main value;
- the user has written a tightly linked action flow;
- a cut would hide a key footfall, tool/prop path, body turn, hand operation, or balance recovery;
- the scene needs immersion or real-time pressure;
- the environment path is important, such as corridor, street crossing, stair, doorway, public space, workplace, stage, classroom walkthrough, or service/process path.

Prefer cutting when:

- one beat needs emphasis that the master shot cannot deliver;
- a small action must be verified, such as contact point, hand operation, object drop, eye-line shift, document mark, product action, or subtle reaction;
- macro geography and detail both matter;
- a reaction changes the meaning of the next action;
- the action crosses a spatial threshold and needs a clean transition.

If both are useful, use a hybrid:

1. master or moving follow to protect continuity;
2. insert or close-up at the decisive point;
3. return to full-body/medium/wide for recovery and consequence.

For short clips under 5 seconds, one-take or two-stage coverage is usually stronger than many cuts. Use more than three cuts only when each cut protects a different viewer need: geography, action readability, proof detail, reaction, or consequence.

For one-take coverage, do not collapse the camera into one generic setting. Divide the one-take into time-slices and give each slice its own camera packet, but write it as natural paragraph prose: shot size, position, focal length, T-stop, frame rate, shutter, support, movement path, focus, and continuity note.

For cut coverage, do not put camera parameters only in the table. Each shot prompt must carry its own camera parameters in paragraph prose because separate cameras or cuts can have different lenses, heights, distances, supports, focus plans, and shutter choices.

## Macro Narrative Plus Detail

Use this pattern when a scene needs both scale and precision.

1. **Macro shot**: establish geography, scale, crowd, weather, room relation, public space, natural space, street, workplace, or institutional space.
2. **Continuity shot**: keep the character's full path or body action readable.
3. **Detail shot**: isolate the exact proof/action the audience must not miss.
4. **Return shot**: show the result in space so the viewer knows what changed.

Do not jump from macro to detail if the viewer has not understood where the detail sits in the larger space.

## Physical Action Coverage

For physical/action/process scenes, cover these in order:

1. movement direction: who moves from where to where;
2. body/readability: feet, hips, shoulders, hand/tool/prop path;
3. contact or near-miss point;
4. reaction/result;
5. recovery/guard/next position.

Do not turn every item above into a separate cut. In many action scenes, one controlled moving shot can cover movement direction, body readability, contact point, and recovery. Cut only when the current camera cannot honestly show the required beat.

Camera choices:

- Full-body wide or medium wide for choreography, running, dance, stunt, demonstration, process movement, or group staging.
- 35mm tracking or lateral dolly for readable human action.
- Over-shoulder or foreground-body/object viewpoint to create relation, pressure, secrecy, or partial information before the full scene opens.
- Controlled orbit-follow for rotations, circular blocking, surrounded pressure, reveal, or power shift. Keep the orbit small enough that viewer orientation does not collapse.
- Low follow for footwork, chase, ground pressure, or slipping/landing.
- 70-100mm insert for contact point, hand grab, foot landing, object hit, document mark, tool use, or product operation.
- Avoid excessive close-ups in action if the body mechanics are the point.
- Avoid excessive full-body safety framing if the scene needs threat, surprise, or impact. Use full-body coverage at the moment body mechanics must be read.

## Dialogue Coverage

Minimum useful coverage:

1. relation/master shot;
2. speaker shot;
3. listener/reaction shot;
4. insert only if a hand/object action changes the meaning.

For dialogue, completeness does not mean many shots. It means eye-line, left/right relation, distance, and reaction are readable.

## Product / Process Coverage

Minimum useful coverage:

1. context shot: why the action is needed;
2. use/action shot: the hand/product/process action;
3. proof shot: what changed;
4. optional packshot or held result if the user asked for commercial coverage.

Do not invent product features, surfaces, lighting, or UI. Use camera coverage to protect the user's existing product action.

## Creative Camera Roles

Use these when they clarify the scene:

- **Over-shoulder / foreground relation frame**: place a shoulder, hand, object, doorway, tool, screen, product, or partial body in foreground to introduce relation or pressure while preserving left/right relation.
- **Long-lens compression**: show danger, crowd, or opponent pressure closing in.
- **Wide lateral move**: show parallel motion, crossing, work path, service path, negotiation distance, or changing relation.
- **Small orbit**: reveal a tactical turn or shift in dominance.
- **Push-in**: concentrate attention on a decision, impact, or realization.
- **Pull-back**: reveal isolation, consequence, or wider scale.
- **Locked-off frame**: make action feel precise, ritualistic, humorous, or inevitable.
- **Low tracking**: protect footwork, running, landing, or ground contact.
- **High/overhead**: show map-like relation or group arrangement, only when geography matters.

Creative camera is not decoration. It must answer: what does this angle let the viewer understand that a normal shot would not?

If a creative shot makes the action harder to follow, remove it. Senior camera direction often means rejecting an attractive angle because it breaks rhythm, screen direction, or viewer orientation.

## Coverage Completeness Checklist

Before finalizing, check:

- Did we preserve the user's original time axis and action order?
- Did we choose the fewest camera changes that still preserve the scene?
- Does every shot or time-slice contain its own camera parameters in readable prose?
- Is there a deliberate viewing-position progression, not just repeated safe full-body coverage?
- Does the viewer understand the macro space if macro space matters?
- Does the viewer see the key body/action/detail that cannot be missed?
- Is the movement direction clear across cuts?
- Is there a reason for every cut or camera move?
- Can any two adjacent shots be merged into one camera move, focus shift, or held frame without losing meaning?
- Are close-ups used for meaning, not random decoration?
- Does the final shot show the consequence or next position?
- Did we avoid adding lighting, art direction, VFX, sound, or choreography?
